Governing AI in East Asia

A comparative study of how Mainland China, Taiwan, and Singapore frame AI development, risk, responsibility, and governance across 15 current and foundational policy instruments.

Corpus15 policy instruments · 5 per jurisdiction
MethodIterative coding + evidence audit

Research at a glance

The same analytical questions were applied across all documents so that similarities and differences could emerge from the coding rather than being assumed in advance. The six dimensions are policy goals, risks, governance tools, actors and roles, regulatory style, and international orientation.

Central comparative finding. Across the selected corpus, AI development and risk governance frequently appear together. The strongest differences are less about whether AI risks are governed and more about how governance is institutionalised, which actors carry responsibility, and which legal or policy instruments are used.

Comparative synthesis

All three selected corpora combine AI development with risk governance and share a growing toolkit that includes risk assessment, human oversight, data governance, transparency, testing and organisational accountability. The main variation lies in how these concerns are institutionalised.

In the selected Mainland China corpus, AI governance increasingly develops operational controls tailored to particular AI system types and capabilities, alongside broader technical and safety frameworks. The Taiwan corpus combines an overarching statutory framework with sectoral, public-sector and existing-law implementation. The Singapore corpus repeatedly operationalises governance through organisational accountability, lifecycle controls, testing and assurance, while maintaining an adaptive and international orientation.

A further cross-cutting pattern is increasing context-specificity: synthetic-content governance foregrounds authenticity and labelling; anthropomorphic systems raise dependency and human-agency concerns; financial AI foregrounds consumer, reliability and sectoral risks; and agentic AI introduces permission boundaries, automation bias, third-party agents and autonomous-action risks.

Methods & codebook

Research design

Five documents were selected for each jurisdiction. Selection prioritised instruments that were current, still in force, or materially relevant to present AI governance at the research cut-off, while retaining a small number of foundational instruments where they remain important to the current governance architecture.

Coding proceeded iteratively: first-round document coding, consolidation of overlapping concepts into a common codebook, second-round consistency recoding, and an evidence audit against official texts or official explanatory materials.

How to read the evidence

Intensity: 2 = primary/strong; 1 = present; 0 = checked and not explicitly identified; — = insufficient evidence or not yet verified.

Evidence status: D = direct policy-document evidence; O = official contextual/explanatory evidence; U = unverified.

Actor presence is separated from actor role. International policy learning, cooperation, interoperability and norm-shaping are also treated as distinct concepts.

Corpus update note. TW5 remains the 4 May 2026 TIPO copyright interpretation selected in the original corpus. Later 2026 work on draft Generative AI Copyright Guidelines is treated as a possible future corpus update rather than silently replacing the submitted document. CN5 is coded conservatively where granular sub-risk claims require full-text verification.

Official sources

Each policy card links to its official government or regulator source. The project uses official texts and official explanatory materials as the primary evidence base.

Limitations

The corpus is purposively selected and is not exhaustive of all AI-related law, policy, standards or sectoral regulation in the three jurisdictions. The instruments differ in legal force, sector and technological focus. Coding therefore describes textual emphasis and institutional design; it does not measure implementation, compliance, regulatory quality, or real-world effectiveness.
